### What makes the script work
1. **Rule collision, not trivia.** Loyalty assumes the owner exists in the world. The hook removes exactly that.
2. **The baseline line (beat 2)** turns an expert question into a universal one, so even casual players can predict.
3. **Two questions** = two payoffs. The first answer is half-expected (yes, it kills), the second is the weird
   one (it waits, then comes home). Expected → weird is the order.
4. **Proof shots**: the chat death message is quoted word for word, which reads as "real test, not made up".
5. **Ends on the reveal.** Nothing to skip at the end → ~94 % average view duration → loops.
6. Title restates the hook with a caps keyword (LEAVE) and "???".

## B. The creator's breakdown: "I made $1,991 from a Single Minecraft short.. (just copy me)"

  The speaker presents the trident short as their own and walks through its analytics and how they make these.

### Their points, summarised
- **Stats**: stayed-to-watch ~83-84 % from day one and it *held* through the whole push; normally it drops as
  reach widens. The goal is "appeal to as many people as possible". AVD ~29 s.
- **Length**: ~30 s helped, since a slightly longer short reads as "more content" to the algorithm (their opinion).
- **Idea > execution, but both**: in a crowded niche, YouTube pushes ideas that fill a gap ("has this exact idea
  been done?"). The hook "if you do this, what will happen?" is "all you need to learn"; the rest explains the
  answer in an engaging way. The "if you do this" part must be something people never wondered about.
- **Two identical players** on screen, like clone/comparison videos, which they say helped.
- **Replay Mod** for camera paths: few Minecraft creators use it well, so it sets the video apart.
- **Music**: one intrigue/"what's going to happen?" track reused across videos (they name it as "I Think I Was
  There"; verify title + license). **SFX**: a "rev" hit and small sound accents add "pizzazz".
- **Clip selection** (most important in the intro):
  - many cuts in the first seconds, aim for **< 1 s per cut** without overloading;
  - an approach shot toward the subject ("getting into the video");
  - a top-down shot sliding over the player (seen in movies);
  - if the video hints at a variation (walling yourself off), show it, then explain/answer it to "satisfy the viewer";
  - fast-forward timelapse building clips make strong hook visuals (e.g. "a wall of leaves around a village").
